## How to bring your company in line with the ENS, step by step

If you work with Spanish public administration — or want to start — there are three letters that will show up in your tender documents sooner or later: **ENS**.

And no, it is not a formality you settle with a couple of PDFs and good intentions. The **National Security Framework** is a real cybersecurity project that, done well, leaves your company far better protected. Done badly, it turns into a sink for time and money.

If you want to explore this approach further, you can also look at our [GRC service: governance, risk and compliance](/en/solutions/grc/).

The good news? It boils down to **9 steps**. Let's get to it.

## The 9 steps to ENS compliance

### 1. Define the scope

First step, and the one most people skip. Mistake.

You do not have to apply the ENS to your entire company. Only to what is involved in the contracts that require it: the services, systems, data and suppliers that are part of that relationship with public administration.

No more, because you would inflate the project for nothing. No less, because the audit will notice.

**Quick example:** you build a platform for a ministry. That platform, its infrastructure and its cloud providers are in scope. Your internal CRM for private clients is not.

### 2. Run a gap analysis

With the scope settled, it is time to look in the mirror.

The **gap analysis** compares where you are with what the ENS asks for: what you already have, what is half done and what is missing entirely.

Typically you discover that the technical side is not doing badly, but that documentation, formal roles and supplier management leave quite a gap.

No need to panic: that is exactly what a diagnosis is for.

### 3. Categorise the system

The ENS classifies systems into three categories:

- **Basic**
- **Medium**
- **High**

The category depends on the impact an incident would have across five dimensions:

- Confidentiality
- Integrity
- Availability
- Authenticity
- Traceability

The dimension with the highest impact sets the overall category. And that category defines how many controls you will have to meet.

A **High** category system carries considerably more requirements than a **Basic** one, so it pays to be honest here without being catastrophic.

### 4. Analyse the risks

This is where formal methodology comes in. The most common one in the ENS world is **MAGERIT**, although there are valid alternatives.

The idea is to identify:

- Your critical assets.
- The real threats.
- The vulnerabilities that could be exploited.
- The impact of an incident.
- The risks worth prioritising.

We are talking about risks ranging from **ransomware** to a laptop somebody leaves behind in a taxi or on a plane.

This analysis lets you decide where to invest first, because the budget is rarely infinite.

### 5. Design the adequacy plan

With the diagnosis and the risks on the table, it is time to build the plan.

ENS measures fall into three broad blocks:

#### Organisational measures

Policies, roles, committees and responsibilities.

It sounds bureaucratic, but without governance the technical measures are left orphaned.

#### Technical measures

Encryption, access control, backups, hardening and system protection.

This is the part that usually feels most natural to technical teams.

#### Operational measures

Monitoring, incident management, maintenance and secure day-to-day operation.

This is where you see whether security is genuinely embedded or only exists on paper.

### 6. Implement the measures

The most intense phase.

Time to move from paper to action:

- Multi-factor authentication.
- Detection tooling.
- Operating procedures.
- Staff training.
- Stronger incident management.
- Better control over suppliers.

Depending on the company's situation, it can make sense to lean on solutions such as **SIEM**, **EDR** or an external **SOC** service if there is not enough in-house capacity.

One piece of advice: do not try to do everything at once. Prioritise whatever closes the highest risks and move forward in phases.

It may also help to review our analysis of [XDR vs SIEM: which solution fits your business](/en/blog/xdr-vs-siem-la-solucion-para-tu-negocio/), particularly when the company needs to mature its visibility and response capabilities.

### 7. Collect evidence

The golden rule of any audit: **what cannot be demonstrated does not exist**.

You have to store evidence from day one:

- Logs.
- Backup reports.
- Signed policies.
- Training records.
- Committee minutes.
- Access reviews.
- Incident reports.
- Evidence of implemented controls.

Do not wait until the audit is upon you to start hunting for documents in drawers.

### 8. Declaration or certification

The final exam depends on the system's category.

#### Basic category systems

Conformity is demonstrated through a **Declaration of Conformity**, based on a documented self-assessment.

#### Medium or high category systems

A **Certification of Conformity** is required, following an external audit.

Not every ENS project ends in the same kind of validation, and understanding this difference from the start avoids misplaced expectations.

### 9. Continuous maintenance

Getting the seal is not the end.

The ENS demands a living cycle:

- Risk review.
- Updating measures.
- Tracking technology changes.
- Supplier review.
- Periodic audits.
- Continuous improvement.

Companies that treat the ENS as a project with a closing date tend to get unpleasant surprises at renewal.

## 4 mistakes we see constantly

| The mistake | The consequence |
|---|---|
| Starting to write documents before defining the scope | Generic policies that have to be redone |
| Believing it is only an IT matter | Without management, HR, Legal and Procurement, organisational controls stay in the drawer |
| Forgetting the suppliers | Many incidents start in third parties or cloud environments that nobody put in scope |
| Treating it as a one-off event | Without maintenance, the certification loses value and the risks come back |

## ENS + ISO 27001 + NIS2: better together

If you already know **ISO 27001** or have **NIS2** on the horizon, good news: the three frameworks share a lot of DNA.

The key is to think about integration from the start, rather than trying to fit pieces together afterwards.

A well-planned approach lets you:

- Reuse controls.
- Take advantage of existing evidence.
- Avoid duplication.
- Build a more coherent GRC strategy.
