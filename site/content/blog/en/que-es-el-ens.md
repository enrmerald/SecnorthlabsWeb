The **National Security Framework**, commonly known by its Spanish acronym **ENS**, is the regulatory framework setting out the security requirements that information systems linked to the Spanish public sector have to meet.

Its purpose is to guarantee that digital services are secure, reliable and protected against incidents that could affect information, citizens or the running of public administration.

Although it is often read as an obligation exclusive to the public sector, the ENS can also affect private companies that provide services to public administration, develop systems for public bodies or handle information within public contracts.

Understanding what the ENS is therefore matters more and more for any company that wants to work with the public sector or improve its position in tenders.

If you want a broader view of when it applies and how to approach it, you can also read our [ENS guide: when it is mandatory and how to comply](/en/blog/ens-cuando-es-obligatorio-y-como-adaptarse/).

## What the National Security Framework is

The **ENS** is Spanish regulation defining the principles, requirements and security measures that must be applied to information systems used in the field of electronic government.

The framework currently in force is governed by **Royal Decree 311/2022**, which replaced the earlier Royal Decree 3/2010 and updated the security approach for new scenarios such as cloud, remote work, mobility, system interconnection and the provision of digital services by third parties.

In practical terms, the ENS helps answer questions such as:

- Which information has to be protected.
- Which risks can affect a system.
- Which security measures have to be implemented.
- What responsibilities each role in the organisation has to take on.
- How to demonstrate that the system meets the required standards.

The ENS is not limited to installing security tooling. It demands orderly security management, combining organisational, technical and operational measures.

## What the ENS is for

The ENS exists to establish a common baseline of security across digital services related to the public sector.

Its main purpose is to protect information and ensure that systems can keep working correctly even in the face of errors, incidents or cyberattacks.

Its most important objectives include:

- Protecting information against unauthorised access.
- Guaranteeing the availability of digital services.
- Preserving the integrity of data.
- Ensuring the authenticity of users, systems and operations.
- Enabling the traceability of the actions carried out.
- Reducing the impact of security incidents.
- Improving the capacity to prevent, detect and respond.

In that sense, the ENS should not be understood solely as a legal obligation, but as a tool for improving **enterprise cybersecurity** and strengthening trust in digital services.

## The five security dimensions of the ENS

One of the key elements of the ENS is that security is analysed across several dimensions.

These dimensions make it possible to assess the impact an incident would have on an information system:

### Confidentiality

Guarantees that information is only accessible to authorised people, systems or entities.

It prevents, for example, sensitive data belonging to an administration or a citizen from being read by unauthorised third parties.

### Integrity

Ensures that information is not modified improperly, whether accidentally or maliciously.

An integrity failure can lead to wrong decisions, lost trust or legal problems.

### Availability

Aims for systems and services to be accessible when they are needed.

This dimension is especially important in digital public services, critical platforms or systems supporting administrative processes.

### Authenticity

Makes it possible to verify that a person, system or entity really is who they claim to be.

It relates to mechanisms such as authentication, user identification, digital certificates and access control.

### Traceability

Makes it possible to know what happened, when, how and who was involved in a given action.

Traceability is key to investigating incidents, detecting anomalous behaviour and demonstrating compliance in audits.

## Why the ENS is mandatory

The ENS is mandatory for public sector bodies within its scope of application.

Its impact, though, is not limited to public administration. It can also affect private companies when they take part in delivering public services or manage systems and information related to administration.

In practice, a private company may need to comply with the ENS when:

- It provides technology services to a public administration.
- It develops, maintains or hosts systems used by public bodies.
- It handles information linked to public services.
- It takes part in tenders where ENS compliance is required.
- It forms part of the supply chain of a digital public service.
- The contract or specification explicitly sets ENS conformity requirements.

This means that the ENS does not apply simply because you "work with an administration", but because of the type of service provided, the system affected, the information processed and the specific requirements of the contract.

Analysing the scope properly before starting a compliance process is therefore important.

## The ENS and private companies: a point that often raises doubts

One of the most common questions is whether a private company is obliged to comply with the ENS.

The answer depends on the context.

A company that does not provide services to the public sector and does not handle information linked to administration may not be directly bound by the ENS.

If that same company, however, builds a platform for a town council, hosts information belonging to a public body, provides technical support on an administrative system or takes part in a tender where the ENS is required, the situation changes.

In those cases, the ENS can become a contractual or technical-solvency requirement.

So the right question is not only:

"Is my company obliged to comply with the ENS?"

But:

"Do any of my systems, services or contracts fall within the scope of the ENS?"

## How the ENS applies to information systems

Another frequent mistake is to think the ENS applies to the whole company in a generic way.

In reality, the ENS applies to **specific information systems**.

The same company can have several systems with different levels of requirement. For example:

- An informational corporate website.
- A SaaS platform used by an administration.
- An internal document management system.
- An application processing citizen data.
- Cloud infrastructure supporting public services.

Each system has to be analysed according to the information it processes, the services it provides and the impact a security incident would have.

Based on that analysis, the system is classified into one of the three ENS categories:

- Basic category.
- Medium category.
- High category.

That categorisation determines the level of requirement, the applicable measures and the type of conformity assessment needed.

## The difference between ENS compliance, declaration and certification

Three concepts come up when talking about the ENS, and it is worth telling them apart.

### ENS compliance

It means the organisation has implemented the necessary security measures according to the system, its category and its risk analysis.

It is not only about having documentation, but about genuinely applying the corresponding organisational, technical and operational measures.

### Declaration of conformity

Usually associated with basic category systems.

Here the organisation can carry out a self-assessment and issue a declaration of conformity, provided the system meets the applicable requirements.

### Certification of conformity

Applies to medium or high category systems.

In those cases, conformity has to be verified through a formal audit carried out by an accredited body.

This distinction matters because not every system requires the same effort, and not every ENS project necessarily ends in external certification.

## Which measures the ENS includes

The ENS covers security measures of different kinds.

They are not only technological. They also include governance, organisation, risk management, operation, protection and incident response.

In summary, they can be grouped into three broad blocks:

### Organisational measures

Policies, roles, responsibilities, internal procedures and security governance.

For example:

- A security policy.
- Assignment of responsible roles.
- Risk management.
- Internal rules.
- Review and improvement procedures.

### Technical measures

Measures related to the technological protection of systems.

For example:

- Access control.
- Authentication.
- Encryption.
- Network segmentation.
- Backups.
- Protection against malware.
- Event monitoring.
- Vulnerability management.

### Operational measures

Measures that keep security running day to day.

For example:

- Incident management.
- Activity logging.
- Log review.
- Service continuity.
- Secure maintenance.
- Internal audits.
- Supplier monitoring.

In organisations with a degree of technological complexity, solutions such as [SIEM and SOAR](/en/blog/que-es-un-siem-y-para-que-sirve-y-un-soar/), [SOC services](/en/blog/que-es-un-soc-en-ciberseguridad/) or advanced detection platforms can help meet the monitoring, traceability and response requirements more effectively.

## ENS, ISO 27001 and NIS2: related frameworks, but not the same

The ENS coexists with other cybersecurity frameworks and regulations, such as **ISO 27001** and the **NIS2** directive.

Although they have points in common, they are not equivalent.

**ISO 27001** is an international standard focused on implementing an Information Security Management System.

**NIS2** is a European directive aimed at strengthening cybersecurity in essential and important sectors.

The **ENS**, for its part, is the Spanish framework applicable to information systems linked to the public sector and to the services provided within that scope.

A company can be affected by more than one of these frameworks at the same time.

An organisation might, for instance, use ISO 27001 as its management foundation, fall under NIS2 because of its sector, and need the ENS in order to provide services to public administration.

## What the ENS brings to a company

Although many organisations come to the ENS out of obligation, implementing it can bring significant benefits.

The main ones include:

- Greater control over information systems.
- Reduced security risk.
- Better internal organisation.
- Greater trust from clients, suppliers and administrations.
- Better preparation for audits and tenders.
- The ability to demonstrate cybersecurity maturity.
- Alignment with other compliance frameworks.
- Improved incident response.

From a business perspective, the ENS can become a competitive advantage.

It does not only allow you to meet regulatory or contractual requirements: it demonstrates that the company manages security seriously, in a structured and verifiable way.

## Common mistakes in interpreting the ENS

Several mistakes of approach come up regularly in ENS compliance projects.

### Thinking the ENS only affects the public sector

Although it originates in the public sector, many private companies can be affected when they provide services or handle information for administration.

### Believing documentation is enough

Documentation is necessary, but not sufficient.

The ENS demands real measures, implemented and maintained over time.

### Applying it to the company without analysing specific systems

The ENS category is determined per information system, not per company as a whole.

### Confusing declaration with certification

Not every system requires external certification, but medium and high category systems do need a formal audit.

### Treating it as a one-off project

The ENS requires review, follow-up and continuous improvement.

Security does not end when a declaration or certification is obtained.

## How to start if your company needs to comply with the ENS

The first step should not be drafting documents in isolation, or implementing tools without a rationale.

The sensible approach is to start with an initial analysis that answers several questions:

- Which services the company provides.
- Which contracts or tenders require the ENS.
- Which information systems are affected.
- Which information is processed.
- Which category might apply.
- Which measures already exist.
- Which gaps have to be closed.
- How much effort compliance would take.

From there you can define a realistic, prioritised roadmap suited to the business.

This approach avoids oversizing the project, reduces mistakes in the audit and lets you progress more efficiently.

## Frequently asked questions about the ENS

### What does ENS mean?

ENS stands for **Esquema Nacional de Seguridad**, Spain's National Security Framework.

It is the framework governing the security requirements applicable to public sector information systems and to certain systems belonging to companies that provide services to administration.

### Is the ENS mandatory for every company?

Not for every company.

It is mandatory for the public sector, and it can be mandatory for private companies when they provide services to administration, handle public information or take part in contracts where ENS compliance is required.

### Does the ENS apply to the whole company?

Not necessarily.

The ENS applies to specific information systems. A company can have several systems, and each one can have a different category.

### Which categories exist in the ENS?

There are three categories: basic, medium and high.

The category depends on the impact an incident would have across dimensions such as confidentiality, integrity, availability, authenticity and traceability.

### What is the difference between an ENS declaration and certification?

The declaration of conformity usually applies to basic category systems, through self-assessment.

The certification of conformity applies to medium or high category systems and requires a formal audit by an accredited body.

### Does the ENS replace ISO 27001?

No.

The ENS and ISO 27001 are different frameworks, although they can complement each other. ISO 27001 focuses on an information security management system, while the ENS sets specific requirements for systems linked to the Spanish public sector.

### What happens if a company does not comply with the ENS?

If the ENS is required in a contract or tender, failing to comply can prevent you from accessing the project, create contractual breaches or complicate the relationship with administration.

A lack of adequate measures also increases the risk of security incidents.
